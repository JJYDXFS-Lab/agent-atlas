title: Design and organizational decision-making
---

How do systems pursue goals with limited resources, and how do organizations shape their choices? This route begins with selected concepts from Herbert A. Simon's _The Sciences of the Artificial_ (third edition, 1996) and _Administrative Behavior_ (fourth edition, 1997), then compares perspectives from Hutchins, Suchman, Hollnagel and Woods, and AIMA. Each entry identifies its source and scope; the route does not merge these perspectives into one theory.

## Design under constraints

- [[artifact-interface|Artifact interface]] — the relation among a system's organization, environment, and purpose.
- [[bounded-rationality|Bounded rationality]] — limits on knowledge, anticipation, attention, and alternatives considered.
- [[satisficing|Satisficing]] — searching for an alternative that meets stated standards of acceptability.
- [[means-ends-analysis|Means–ends analysis]] — selecting actions by relating state differences to relevant operators.
- [[near-decomposability|Near decomposability]] — stronger within-subsystem interactions and weaker, slower cross-subsystem effects.

## Organizational choice

- [[fact-value-distinction|Fact–value distinction]] — separating empirical premises from preferred ends in Simon's framework.
- [[decision-premises|Decision premises]] — how information and commitments structure a choice.
- [[organizational-authority|Organizational authority]] — accepting another's decision as a premise, with Simon's qualifications concerning expertise and persuasion.

Suggested order: read bounded rationality before satisficing, because Simon's commentary presents satisficing and a simplified world model as the two features of bounded rationality. Read the fact–value distinction before decision premises, and decision premises before organizational authority. When comparing these entries with [[agent|Agent]], [[policy|Policy]], or [[context-window|Context window]], compare the questions each one answers rather than treating the definitions as equivalent. A model-input limit, for example, is narrower than Simon's limits on attention and on the alternatives considered.

Continue to [[agent-foundations|Agent foundations]] for perception–action and POMDP formulations, or [[memory-and-information|Memory and information]] for retained and retrieved information. The agent examples are our own illustrative applications. They are not attributed to Simon and do not imply that he wrote about contemporary LLM assistants.

Read [[problem-representation|Problem representation]] for how a representation structures operations and organization, then [[situated-action|Situated action]] for the practical work through which plans are realized. ## Changing work, records, and responsibility

[[substitution-myth|Substitution myth]] asks what other work changes when technology is introduced. [[human-machine-reconfiguration|Human–machine reconfiguration]] adds questions about enacted boundaries, invisible labor, and responsibility. Suchman examines records as resources for coordination and for making work visible to management; these uses can conflict, and fuller recording can constrain discretion. Her later cases make maintenance and supporting performance part of the account of apparent autonomy. (Chapters 11 and 15, printed pp. 202–205, 277–286)[^1]

For an Atlas application, assess who verifies, repairs, and maintains an assistant as well as who performs the visible task. A permission mechanism does not exhaust these relational responsibilities. [[assistance-game|Assistance game]] makes shared payoff and asymmetric preference information explicit formal assumptions; it does not settle the legitimacy of those goals or boundaries.
