# Joint cognitive system

Hollnagel and Woods treat a **joint cognitive system** as a functional unit of cooperating participants and artefacts, analysed through the work it accomplishes and its ability to maintain control under disturbances. Physical packaging does not determine the unit: its boundary depends on the function and the purpose of analysis. (Chapters 1 and 3, pp. 19–23, 67–69)[^j]

## Coagency, boundaries, and control

Coagency concerns working together. When deciding what belongs inside the system, the authors distinguish what matters to performance from what can effectively be controlled. An important external dependency can remain part of the environment. Control includes timely prevention, compensation, and recovery, rather than only issuing a command. (Chapter 6, pp. 115–117; Chapter 7, pp. 135–138)[^j]

For an Atlas example, a researcher, assistant, and retrieval interface may form the unit when evaluating the timely production of a defensible answer. A remote archive outage remains a disturbance unless the ensemble can control it. Enlarging the unit exposes dependencies but can obscure who is responsible for particular actions.

## Human participation and the word cognitive

The book's main domain is human-inclusive systems. Its wording is not fully uniform: p. 22 says a cognitive system always includes a human, while p. 23 allows sophisticated machine–machine cooperation in principle; the generic rule on p. 68 is functional rather than material. These passages support a qualified extension, not an absolute exclusion of machine-only systems or a finding that current LLM pairs qualify. The authors also distinguish being called cognitive from possessing cognition. (pp. 22–23, 68, 139)[^j] Neither the label nor joint performance establishes rights, consent, or permission to act.
