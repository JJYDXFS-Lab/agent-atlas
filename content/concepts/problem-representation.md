# Problem representation

In Simon's account of design, a **problem representation** structures the states, relations, and operations through which a problem can be understood and worked on. Changing representation can make different operations or organizations possible; it is more than choosing a display format for an already settled calculation. (Chapter 5, pp. 131–134; Chapter 6, pp. 141–146)[^c]

## Representation and organization in Simon

Number scrabble and tic-tac-toe illustrate how related constraints become easier to see in different representations. In Simon's account of the Economic Cooperation Administration, alternative representations of the task lead to different programs and organizational arrangements. Representation helps select the work and who does it. (pp. 131–134, 141–143)[^c]

A shared representation can organize discussion even when its numerical estimates remain weak. Simon's emissions-policy example distinguishes a cost-benefit framework's coordinating role from the reliability of the numbers available within it. Naming uncertainty does not improve the evidence. (pp. 145–147)[^c] Coordination value and empirical validity therefore need separate assessment.

## Hutchins: representational task transformation

Hutchins follows a different mechanism: an overall input–output constraint can remain while the representations used change the local operations people must perform. Navigation's alternative methods of determining speed redistribute calculation among people and artefacts. This comparison is an editorial connection, not a claim that Hutchins and Simon propose an identical mechanism. (Chapter 3, pp. 147–155)[^h]

### Precomputation

Earlier work can transform later computation by preparing a representation or arrangement that makes subsequent operations simpler. Such transformations rely on conditions that must still hold when the representation is used. The three-minute navigation rule employs an approximate nautical convention, not an exact unit identity; a more convenient local operation does not by itself establish lower total cost. (pp. 159–168)[^h]

## Generating, testing, and allocating search

Simon distinguishes evaluating a completed design from estimating whether further search down a partial path is worthwhile. Search has opportunity costs, and estimates used to allocate it can themselves be weak. Dividing and ordering generation and testing also shapes the resulting design: working from a house's outside inward need not produce the same style as working from its interior outward. (Chapter 5, pp. 124–130)[^c] These mechanisms belong here without assuming that generation and testing are independent modules.

## Application and related concepts

An Atlas example is to represent a review task as a source-to-claim table rather than a prose draft. This changes which missing links are visible and what checking remains; it does not guarantee the claims are true or the whole workflow cheaper.
