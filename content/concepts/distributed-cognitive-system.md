# Distributed cognitive system

In Hutchins's analysis, cognitive processes can be studied across a system of people, instruments, and other media. The explanatory task is to follow how representational states propagate and transform within a specified system, rather than attributing its performance to a single person's mental operations. The boundary is an analytical choice tied to the task. (Chapters 2–3, pp. 49–51, 117–129; Chapter 9, pp. 373–374)[^h]

## Representational work and attribution

In ship navigation, a bearing moves through an instrument, spoken report, written record, and chart. These are transformations with different constraints, not simply copies of a message. A position that becomes available through plotting need not already have been represented by any individual. The system's computational properties therefore cannot automatically be assigned to each member. (Chapter 3, pp. 117–129, 157–158)[^h] [[problem-representation|Problem representation]] compares this account of task transformation with Simon's account of choosing a problem representation.

## Communication coupling and interpretive diversity

Hutchins's simplified simulations explore how communication coupling affects the preservation and convergence of interpretations. Strong early coupling can eliminate useful diversity, but isolation or delayed integration can also leave disagreement or an inconsistent combination. Shared upstream inputs can produce correlated errors; agreement downstream is not independent corroboration of that input. (Chapter 5, pp. 243–262)[^h] These simulations do not establish an optimal communication schedule for LLM teams.

## Local adaptation and retention

During a navigation failure, local adjustments in information and work distribution gradually form an effective organization. A global explicit plan need not precede this adaptation. The shared heading called “total” was named after the division of work had begun to stabilize; naming it is not evidence that the name caused the improvement. The final arrangement was not preserved. Successful repair, organizational change, and durable retention are different claims. (Chapter 8, pp. 317–351, especially 340–351)[^h]

Hutchins's **computational ecology** concerns the mutual fit of tools, practices, and accumulated structures, including their current interdependence; it does not require weak coupling. His discussion of **metamediation** concerns organizing the use of mediating structures: the spatial arrangement of a list does not itself execute the procedure. Learning and reconstruction remain work performed in the system, rather than literal transfer of an external object into a mind. (Chapters 2–3, pp. 112–114, 168; Chapter 7, pp. 296–300, 303–314)[^h]

## Boundaries and application

[[joint-cognitive-system|Joint cognitive system]] emphasizes joint function and timely control; a successful account of distributed computation does not alone show adequate recovery from disturbance. [[near-decomposability|Near decomposability]] requires particular coupling strengths and timescales; distribution alone does not establish it. [[horizon-of-observation|Horizon of observation]] concerns the access needed to detect and learn from others' work, rather than guaranteed [[common-ground|Common ground]].

An Atlas application is to evaluate a researcher, assistant, retrieval tool, and evidence table as a system while separately measuring the assistant alone. State the attribution unit and inspect transformations between sources and conclusions. This is a proposed analytical use, not an empirical result about contemporary agents or proof that more workers improve performance.
