# Situated action

In Suchman's account, **situated action** draws attention to how action is accomplished through the particular material and social circumstances in which it unfolds. Plans describe and orient action, but do not specify in advance all the practical work required to carry them out. This is an account of the relation between plans and activity, not a recommendation to avoid planning. (Chapters 1–2, pp. 13–22; Chapters 5–6, pp. 58–61, 70–74)[^s]

## Plans as resources

A plan can support anticipation and coordination while leaving its actual realization dependent on skills, judgments, and circumstances that the plan does not exhaust. Suchman's canoe example makes this dependence visible: planning the passage and accomplishing it are different activities, and successful action is not merely passive reaction after a plan fails. Plans can also help people account for an action afterward. (Chapters 5–6, pp. 58–61, 70–74)[^s]

## Interaction and practical understanding

The copier study examines how users make sense of instructions amid their own developing task. Recognizing a machine-observable event does not settle what that event means in the user's activity. The study's conclusions distinguish improving access, making limitations intelligible, and supporting the surrounding work. (Chapter 10, pp. 179–186)[^s] See [[asymmetric-access-to-interactional-resources|Asymmetric access to interactional resources (analyst term)]].

## Comparison and application

For a complementary question about how participants establish and revise the business they are doing together, follow [[joint-project|Joint project]]. Its account of proposals and responses offers a focused comparison with situated action, rather than replacing Suchman's account of plans and circumstances.

[[means-ends-analysis|Means–ends analysis]] describes Simon's selective search procedure; situated action asks what realizing a plan requires in practice. Suchman's critique should not replace Simon's own account: [[problem-representation|Problem representation]] and [[decision-premises|Decision premises]] also address how problems and choices are organized. The perspectives differ without reducing Simon to inflexible advance planning.

For an Atlas illustration, a data-cleaning plan may say “resolve duplicate records.” Applying it requires deciding what counts as the same entity, recognizing exceptions, and obtaining missing context. Record that situated work when assessing the plan's adequacy. This example is a contemporary application, not evidence from the copier study about LLM capabilities. [[human-machine-reconfiguration|Human–machine reconfiguration]] extends the inquiry to how boundaries and responsibilities are enacted.
