# Substitution myth

Hollnagel and Woods call the assumption that an artefact can be introduced or replaced while the rest of the work system stays unchanged the **substitution myth**. Automation can change tasks, dependencies, adaptation, and failure paths. A component improvement therefore does not alone demonstrate a system improvement. (Chapter 5, pp. 101–108)[^j]

## Changing work around the artefact

The copier discussion distinguishes intended function from the work created around exceptions and new capabilities. People and organizations adapt to the changed artefact, and task design must consider that adaptation. The authors' adaptation examples illustrate the argument; they do not establish a universal convergence time or prove that all benefits will be consumed by increased demand. (pp. 101–108)[^j]

The **envisioned world problem**, discussed through Woods's earlier work, concerns artefacts changing the circumstances assumed in their design. This is a bridge to evaluating future work, not a prediction of any single deployment's effects. (Chapter 9, p. 181)[^j]

## Forced automaton analogy

The authors' **forced automaton** analogy criticizes interfaces that constrain people to anticipated interaction sequences. Such constraints may exclude legitimate unanticipated work. This is a related mechanism, not another definition of substitution. The claim that finite machine inputs force people to be regarded as automata is too broad to adopt as a general conclusion: the same chapter recognizes protective barriers. Whether a restriction is appropriate depends on its purpose and effects. (pp. 183–185, 188–189)[^j]

## Application and comparisons

An illustrative drafting assistant may save writing time while creating provenance checks, exception handling, or extra review work. Measure those changes along with output speed, retaining the possibility of a net improvement. These are Atlas evaluation questions, not evidence that LLM substitution is necessarily harmful or that deskilling must occur.

[[artifact-interface|Artifact interface]] concerns fit with purpose and environment; substitution asks how intervention changes that fit. [[near-decomposability|Near decomposability]] can justify limited spillover only under appropriate interaction conditions. [[human-machine-reconfiguration|Human–machine reconfiguration]] also examines changes around technology but gives distinct attention to enacted boundaries, political differences, and responsibility.
