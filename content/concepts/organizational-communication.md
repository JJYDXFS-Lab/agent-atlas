# Organizational communication

In Simon's administrative analysis, **organizational communication** makes information and other decision premises available across an organization. The issue is not just whether messages travel, but which information is reported, screened, suppressed, or combined, and where decisions are made. (Chapter VIII, pp. 208–219)[^b]

## Distributed information and reporting rules

A lathe operator encounters a breakdown locally; an insurance file brings case information into relation with organizational rules. These examples distinguish where relevant information arises from where it is used. Reporting rules determine what must be communicated, while manuals can carry relatively stable premises in the other direction. (pp. 209–213)[^b]

Upward suppression, staff screening, liaison, and independent accounting or inspection channels alter what reaches a decision-maker. Screening can conserve attention while excluding consequential evidence. An independent channel offers another route, not an all-seeing account of the organization. (pp. 215–219)[^b]

## Boundaries and application

[[decision-premises|Decision premises]] concerns what actually structures a choice; transmission alone does not prove reception or acceptance. [[organizational-authority|Organizational authority]] concerns acceptance based on another's decision. [[conversational-grounding|Conversational grounding]] concerns evidence of sufficient mutual understanding, a distinct question from organizational information routing.

For an Atlas illustration, a research assistant's source summary may filter out caveats before a reviewer sees it. Record reporting criteria and material omissions, and preserve access to underlying evidence when appropriate. A complete message log cannot reveal influences that were never expressed or information that was suppressed. This application is not a claim that Simon's historical examples validate a particular modern audit workflow.
