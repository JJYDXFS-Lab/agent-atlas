# Horizon of observation

Hutchins uses **horizon of observation** for the range of task activity available to a participant's observation. This distribution of access helps explain opportunities for error detection and learning in a navigation team. Availability is only one condition: knowledge, expectations, attention, and perspective also affect what a person can recognize. (Chapter 6, pp. 268–279)[^h]

## Seeing, detecting, and learning

A participant can encounter others' actions and their consequences without being able to diagnose them. An experienced navigator's detection of an error and a novice's opportunity to learn from the same activity are different outcomes. Wider visibility may expose useful relationships, but can also distract participants and lead to further errors; Hutchins does not quantify this tradeoff. (p. 279)[^h] More visible activity therefore does not guarantee more learning or better performance. (pp. 268–279)[^h]

Tool design and work organization shape this horizon. Hutchins contrasts chart work, whose operations are visible on the chart’s surface, with less revealing calculator use. A discussion between a plotter and bearing recorder also lets a nearby learner follow how landmarks are selected; that opportunity would be lost if the decision were made privately. (pp. 269–271)[^h]

## Related concepts

[[joint-salience|Joint salience]] asks which coordination solution stands out against what participants take to be shared. This provides a useful next question after examining observational access: even if both can see several candidate objects, which one can they identify together?

[[observation|Observation]] in this Atlas uses a specified POMDP formulation; a horizon of observation describes access within organized practice and is not itself an observation probability distribution. [[common-ground|Common ground]] requires a further account of what participants take to be shared. [[asymmetric-access-to-interactional-resources|Asymmetric access to interactional resources (analyst term)]] emphasizes unequal access to the resources and relevance of an unfolding interaction; Hutchins's emphasis here is detection and learning opportunity.

[[heeding-practices|Heeding practices]] adds a focus on domain-specific monitoring, displaying, and expectations of competent conduct; it complements the distinction here between access and recognition.

## Application

For an illustrative agent review workflow, give the reviewer the source evidence and the transformations needed to inspect a claim, then test whether they can actually detect a seeded error. Supplying a longer transcript is not proof of diagnostic access. The example is an Atlas proposal, not an experimental finding about LLM reviewers.
