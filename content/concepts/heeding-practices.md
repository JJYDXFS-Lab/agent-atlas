# Heeding practices

BSCW let users see traces of document activity, such as another user's reading, renaming, or moving a document. Schmidt treats this as an early form of support for mutual awareness, while asking what it leaves out about skilled cooperation. An event record does not yet specify which events matter to a practitioner, how they should be recognized, or how colleagues make relevant activity noticeable. (Chapter 11, pp. 351–354)[^1]

**Heeding practices**, in Schmidt's account, are the domain-specific practices through which people skillfully **monitor for** relevant cues and **display** aspects of their own work for colleagues. They are practical skills developed through training and experience, not simply a mental state produced by making information visible. (pp. 353–354)[^1]

## Monitoring for and displaying

Monitoring for is selective: practitioners attend to categories of events relevant to this work and setting. Displaying is its complementary side: they make their own activity available in ways colleagues can use. These techniques can be integrated into ongoing work so thoroughly that competent performance takes little noticeable extra effort. Their apparent ease does not make them generic abilities. (pp. 351, 353–354)[^1]

The practices are also conventional and accountable. Schmidt's illustrative rebuke, “Are you asleep?”, expresses an expectation that a colleague should have noticed what was happening. Supporting these practices therefore involves their domain-specific cues and conventions, not only the propagation of state changes. (p. 354)[^1] This is Schmidt's argument about the design of awareness support, not a blanket dismissal of awareness research.

## Related concepts

[[horizon-of-observation|Horizon of observation]] already distinguishes access from successful recognition and learning. Heeding practices adds a focus on trained monitoring, mutual displaying, and colleagues' expectations of competent conduct. [[articulation-work|Articulation work]] supplies the broader question of coordinating contributions. [[execution-monitoring|Execution monitoring]] examines checks on action, plans, and goals in a planning framework; the objects of analysis differ.

## A contemporary question

In a hypothetical agent-assisted workflow, a generic “active” indicator may tell a colleague less than a well-chosen indication that a required input is missing. Which cues help, when they should be displayed, and what users learn to expect require study in the actual work setting. This analogy neither establishes human heeding skills in a model nor validates every additional notification.
