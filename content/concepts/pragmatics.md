---
title: Pragmatics
---

Pragmatics studies language use in context: how an utterance is interpreted and what a speaker does or conveys by producing it. The boundary with semantics is debated. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy distinguishes **near-side** questions about determining what is said from **far-side** questions about what is accomplished or conveyed beyond saying it [^1].

## Meaning in context

Resolving a reference such as “that file” requires more than a dictionary meaning. Interpreting “Can you close the window?” as a request rather than only a question about ability also depends on the circumstances. These are hypothetical illustrations of different pragmatic tasks; context can settle an interpretation without another explicit instruction.

Ambiguity, vagueness, and missing detail are not interchangeable. An ambiguous expression allows different readings; a vague expression has borderline cases; an instruction can leave details unspecified without having two distinct linguistic readings. Nor does an unstated requirement automatically count as communicated: an inference about speaker meaning needs contextual support.

[[common-ground|Common ground]] concerns what participants treat as shared. [[conversational-grounding|Conversational grounding]] concerns how they establish sufficient understanding. [[theory-of-mind|Theory of mind]] concerns attributing mental states. Each can matter for interpretation, but none is a synonym for pragmatics. Likewise, the conversational situation is broader than the tokens in an LLM's [[context-window|Context window]].

## Interpretation as coordinated action

In _Using Language_, Clark challenges a strict order in which a listener first settles what is said and only then works out what is implicated. His “garage round the corner” example depends on the problem being addressed: running out of petrol favors a service station, while illegal parking favors a parking structure. Inferring the remark's relevance helps resolve the word's meaning, rather than always following completed disambiguation. This is Clark's argument within debates about meaning and implicature. (pp. 144–145)[^2]

His account also gives the addressee a role in settling what an utterance is to count as. “Sit here” can be construed as advice, an offer, or an order; a response displays a construal that the speaker can accept or correct. Sometimes speakers leave several construals open, as a question about accepting credit cards can lead to an answer about which cards are accepted. The resulting **joint construal** is negotiated in the exchange rather than merely read off the sentence or recovered from a fixed initial intention. (pp. 212–214, 218–219)[^2] [[joint-project|Joint project]] develops how this bears on the business participants undertake.

Continue to [[composite-signal|Composite signal]] for meaning formed through descriptions, indications, and demonstrations together, and [[layering-in-language-use|Layering in language use]] for attribution across quotation, pretense, and staged speech. These extend the inquiry beyond interpreting a text string in isolation.
