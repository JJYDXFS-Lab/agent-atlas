# Layering in language use

A teacher reads the opening of _Moby Dick_ to a class. The pupils should not conclude that the teacher—or Melville—is asking to be called Ishmael. Clark uses this example to separate the teacher's delivery, the author–reader activity, and the fictional narrator's address. The same words participate in different actions at different layers. (p. 364)[^1]

**Layering**, in Clark's account, organizes language use across domains of action, each with its participants, roles, place, time, and possible actions. Quotation, fiction, and make-believe can establish a derivative domain through what participants do in a primary one. It is not simply another task contained inside the first task: a fictional character is not literally a participant in the primary scene. (pp. 355–360)[^1]

## Domains and attribution

In Clark's make-believe example, two children handle dirt and pebbles while playing gold miners. A pebble picked up by a child is construed in the derivative domain as a nugget found by a miner. The correspondences develop through agreement, implicit interpretation, and events during play; they can also be disputed. Expressions such as “I,” “here,” and “now” must be interpreted in the currently relevant domain. (pp. 356–358)[^1]

The relation is asymmetric. The players create and have access to their characters' world; the characters, within the pretense, do not similarly create or know the players' world. Stipulating a cabin in the story does not produce a real cabin. Clark presents these as properties of his layering account, not as a claim that performing a fiction has no real-world effects. (pp. 357–360)[^1]

## Staged and ostensible acts

Clark extends layering beyond sustained fiction to brief **staged communicative acts**, including irony and overstatement. In his pretense account of irony, participants stage a serious utterance whose contrast with the actual situation is to be appreciated. This is his account of irony, not a neutral definition shared by every theory. (pp. 371–372, 377–378)[^1]

**Ostensible acts** retain a different ambivalence. In an ostensible invitation, participants make as if an invitation is genuine while recognizing that acceptance is not expected. The recipient's participation in maintaining that appearance distinguishes it from a straightforward sincere invitation or a plainly sarcastic one. Such an invitation can still be misconstrued. (pp. 378, 380, 390)[^1]

## Levels, tracks, and layers

These are three different axes in Clark's account. [[joint-action#levels-of-joint-action|Levels]] distinguish executing, presenting, signaling, and proposing with their paired responses. [[conversational-grounding#projected-evidence-and-tracks|Tracks]] distinguish the ongoing business from collateral communication about that communication. **Layers** distinguish domains in which actions and utterances are attributed. A clarification about how a line was spoken need not create a fictional world; an actor's utterance can itself involve multiple action levels. (pp. 389–391)[^1]

In a tentative Atlas application, a simulated reviewer saying “I approve” belongs to a represented scenario, not automatically to the real workflow's approval process. Identify who supplies wording, whose position it represents, and what actually happens outside the scenario. A real tool operation remains a real operation even when prompted during role-play. This analogy does not settle whether an LLM meets the psychological conditions of Clark's account of joint pretense, nor require that a principal always be a human or an institution.
