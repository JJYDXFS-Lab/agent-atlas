# Joint salience

Asked to choose the same ball as an unknown partner, someone might choose the visually conspicuous basketball. With a friend they regularly play squash with, the squash ball becomes a stronger choice—provided both know who their partner is. Clark's example shows why what stands out to an individual need not be the best coordination solution. (pp. 66–67)[^1]

**Joint salience** is salience relative to participants' current [[common-ground|Common ground]]. Clark proposes that the ideal solution to a coordination problem is the one most prominent on that basis. It is an ideal, not a guarantee of convergence: participants can disagree about their common ground or what is most salient within it. (p. 67)[^1]

## What makes a solution available

An explicit agreement can settle a choice that would otherwise depend on salience. Conventions supply established solutions to recurrent coordination problems, while a local precedent can make one wording stand out. Once maze partners have used “rows” for horizontal passages, reusing it supports coordination on later references. Novel expressions also draw on current common ground: Clark discusses a telephone operator's “ask a zero” as a contextually interpretable reference to someone reached by dialing zero. These are different resources for coordination, not a fixed vocabulary sufficient for every occasion. (pp. 70, 78, 81)[^1]

## Participant-designed coordination problems

When a participant poses a coordination problem, the others can reason that the proposer chose its form, has a solution in mind, and expects them to converge on it. Clark calls these **solvability premises**. The associated **sufficiency premise** is that the presentation, together with relevant common ground, provides enough information. It does not permit adding arbitrary convenient assumptions to make a solution work. These expectations differ from those available for a problem supplied by an unknown third party. (pp. 68–70)[^1]

As a tentative Atlas application, “use the revised table” should be assessed against what both parties can identify as the relevant revision. The assistant's highest retrieval score or newest private copy is not a measure of joint salience. If two versions remain plausible, clarification can establish which revision the participants mean and set a usable precedent. [[conversational-grounding|Conversational grounding]] explains that repair; [[composite-signal|Composite signal]] explains how a description and a jointly attended pointer can work together.
