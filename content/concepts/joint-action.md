# Joint action

Two pianists playing a duet must coordinate their entries, tempo, and phrasing. A recording of one pianist's part might sound the same as a solo performance, yet playing that part with a partner involves different work. Clark uses this example to distinguish **joint action**, performed by an ensemble, from its **participatory actions**, performed by individuals as parts of that joint action. His account treats speaking and listening as such interdependent contributions, rather than independent message production and reception. (pp. 18–21)[^1]

## Roles and goals

Participation does not require identical actions or equal roles. A guide and tourists, or a speaker and an addressee, have different responsibilities. Joint activities also accommodate competition: chess players jointly play a game while each privately aims to win. Clark distinguishes publicly recognized goals that define the activity from private goals, which may conflict with them. Coordination is not a claim of friendliness or aligned interests. (pp. 19, 34–35)[^1]

Roles within speaking can also separate. Clark distinguishes the **vocalizer** who produces the sounds, the **formulator** who composes the wording, and the **principal** whose position and meaning the words represent. A spokesperson reading a prepared statement need not occupy all three roles. These are communicative roles, not a complete allocation of responsibility for the surrounding activity. (pp. 20–21)[^1]

## Levels of joint action

Clark distinguishes at least four simultaneous levels in ordinary conversation. They describe actions in progress, not four stages through which a message passes. The paired contributions are: (pp. 147–149, 152; p. 389)[^1]

| Level | Speaker's contribution         | Addressee's contribution                            |
| ----- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Executing perceptible behavior | Attending to that behavior                          |
| 2     | Presenting a signal            | Identifying the signal                              |
| 3     | Signaling a meaning            | Recognizing what the speaker is to be taken to mean |
| 4     | Proposing a joint project      | Considering the proposal                            |

The initial level-3 formulation on p. 149 is revised through **joint construal**: participants work toward an interpretation they can mutually treat as what the speaker meant, which may differ from the speaker's initial intention. Uptake makes that interpretation available for validation or correction. (pp. 212–213, 218–219)[^1] See [[joint-project|Joint project]]. Level 4 is proposing and considering, not already accepting, authorizing, or completing the proposed project.

**Upward completion** means that success at a higher level depends on completing the lower levels. It does not make their performance a temporal pipeline. **Downward evidence** means that evidence of higher-level success also supports lower-level success; evidence that someone heard a signal does not conversely establish understanding. This is good, defeasible evidence: a seemingly appropriate answer can disclose a misconstrual that the original speaker corrects in a third turn. (pp. 147–148, 152, 234)[^1] [[conversational-grounding|Conversational grounding]] develops the evidence and repair mechanisms.

## Activity state and record

In Clark's chess example, the current board position is the **state**; the moves that produced it form a **trace**. Joint activities accumulate through events participants jointly recognize as advancing them. Public events are not all official moves: adjusting a chess piece or sipping water can be mutually noticed without changing the game. (pp. 40–42)[^1]

The **discourse record** represents the official states and events of the activity. It is part of a broader representation that includes both signals used and the situation being discussed. A trace shows the course taken, not all the alternatives that participants might have considered. (pp. 52–54, 337–338)[^1] In a tentative Atlas application, separate a tool log, the current analysis state, and the decisions participants have placed on record. A log is not automatically a jointly accepted activity record.

## Coordination and timing

Participants coordinate entry into an activity, its ongoing conduct, and exit. Sequence matters, but actions can overlap or remain continuous: a speaker can adjust directions while a partner moves an object. Writing and reading can also be joint actions without simultaneous production and reception; coordination then depends on expectations about the other's processing. (pp. 35–36, 42, 90, 326)[^1] [[joint-salience|Joint salience]] concerns choosing a coordination solution, while [[medium-constraints-on-grounding|Medium constraints on grounding]] concerns the available resources.

This is a different analytical question from selecting an [[action|Action]] in a POMDP or defining a [[distributed-cognitive-system|Distributed cognitive system]] or [[joint-cognitive-system|Joint cognitive system]]. The contemporary tool example is a design analogy, not evidence that an LLM has, or cannot have, the intentions in Clark's human account.
