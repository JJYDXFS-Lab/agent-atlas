# Problem detection

A NICU nurse recalled noticing that a baby looked unwell despite reassuring laboratory results. Changes in color, muscle tone, and the baby's familiar appearance preceded obvious deterioration. Crandall, Klein, and Hoffman use this incident account in their discussion of analyzing cognitive task data. It illustrates a question about skilled recognition; it is not a clinical rule or a claim that every intuition is correct. (Chapter 7, pp. 114–118)[^1]

**Problem detection** concerns noticing that something may be wrong before the problem has been fully specified. _Working Minds_ contrasts this with a laboratory task in which an experimenter has already supplied the problem. (Chapter 8, p. 136)[^1]

**Source scope: provisional.** This entry follows the book's examples and its summary of problem-detection research by Klein and colleagues. Those original studies have not been independently examined for this entry. The account of cues and reframing below is a secondary-source treatment, not a firsthand reconstruction of an original theory.

## Cues and a changing interpretation

The book describes several possibilities: accumulated evidence can cross a threshold; early signs can prompt closer monitoring; or contrary evidence can be explained away until something becomes difficult to dismiss. In some cases a cue prompts a change in the interpretation of a situation, while that changed interpretation also makes the cue's significance clearer. This is the authors' summary of Klein, Pliske, and colleagues' work, not a claim that detection always follows one sequence. (p. 139)[^1]

The NICU account makes the distinction concrete: the nurse's familiarity with this baby mattered to recognizing subtle changes. The account is evidence elicited and interpreted in a study, not transparent access to cognition or a demonstration that subjective certainty should override other evidence. (pp. 114–118)[^1]

## Supporting detection

In the book's account of DC-TRAC, a damage-control display helped users inspect where people and fires were and where they were heading. Its evaluation identified detecting problems as a principal use of the system. Examining what the tool helps people notice can reveal uses beyond its intended role. (Chapter 11, p. 187)[^1]

## Boundaries and a contemporary question

[[execution-monitoring|Execution monitoring]] checks action, plan, and goal compatibility during execution. Such monitoring can reveal a new problem; the distinction here is an emphasis on recognizing and interpreting trouble that has not already been specified. [[problem-representation|Problem representation]] concerns how a problem is structured; detection can occasion a revision of that structure. [[cognitive-requirements|Cognitive requirements]] asks what makes this recognition difficult in a particular task.

In a hypothetical agent-assisted analysis, a reviewer may notice an unexpected pattern before anyone has named the error to test for. Whether a display or alert helps that recognition should be investigated. The book does not establish the same mechanism in LLMs, nor does an assistant's explanation prove why it detected an anomaly.
