# Organizational authority

In Simon's organizational account, **authority** is a relationship in which one person's decision guides another's action. Its exercise depends on behavior and acceptance, not merely a title or a formal organization chart. A communicated decision can become a premise for the recipient's choice without the recipient independently deciding its merits on that occasion. (Chapter VII, printed pp. 178–180)[^1]

## Uses and boundaries

Simon initially distinguishes authority from persuasion and suggestion: the latter work through the recipient's assessment of reasons. He then broadens his usage to include suggestions accepted without critical review, including reliance on expertise. The same pair of people can act through authority on one occasion and through persuasion on another. These qualifications matter when applying his definition. (Chapter VII, printed pp. 179–181)[^1]

This descriptive account does not establish moral legitimacy, legal authority, or a software permission. An agent's tool access is not evidence that a user authorized a particular action. Authority concerns the acceptance of [[decision-premises|Decision premises]]; [[common-ground|Common ground]] concerns shared conversational assumptions and is not an authorization mechanism.

## Operational example

[[joint-project|Joint project]] offers a comparison at the scale of a proposal and response: what is being undertaken, on which interpretation, and with which commitments? Keep that question separate from whether another's decision is accepted as a governing premise in Simon's sense.

An illustrative assistant may accept a user's approved spending ceiling as a governing premise while treating a vendor's recommendation as evidence to evaluate. Record who supplied each instruction and its scope. This is an application to software delegation, not a claim that Simon's analysis of human organizations directly specifies an access-control system.

## Anticipated reactions

Authority need not appear as an explicit command. Simon describes anticipated reactions: a person can adapt a decision to what they expect another will accept, making influence less visible in the record. Acceptance also has limits rather than extending automatically to every subject. (Chapter VII, printed pp. 182–183)[^1] For an Atlas application, an absence of approval messages does not prove an absence of organizational influence; it also does not create software authorization.
