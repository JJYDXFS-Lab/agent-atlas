---
title: Common ground
---

Drawing on earlier accounts, Clark and Brennan describe common ground as participants' mutual knowledge, beliefs, and assumptions (p. 127).[^1] It provides a basis for coordinating what they mean and how they carry out a joint activity. The relevant notion is shared information as participants understand it, not merely a collection of facts available somewhere.

## Shared access and shared understanding

Two collaborators may both have a report without realizing that the other has read it. Conversely, they may jointly assume an outdated deadline. These hypothetical cases distinguish common ground from identical data access and from objective truth. On this reading, the beliefs and assumptions on which communication proceeds can be mistaken.

Suppose a user says “use the revised table.” Whether this reference works depends on which table the participants take to be mutually identifiable. Merely storing the table in [[agent-memory|Agent memory]], or inserting it into a [[context-window|Context window]], does not establish that mutual understanding.

Common ground supplies information for coordination; [[conversational-grounding|grounding]] is the collaborative process of establishing and updating it. Clark and Brennan distinguish coordinating the content of a joint activity from coordinating its process: coordinating on content presupposes common ground, while coordinating on process requires updating it moment by moment (p. 127).[^1] [[theory-of-mind|Theory of mind]] can involve beliefs about another participant's beliefs, but modeling those beliefs is not by itself evidence that something is mutually understood.

## Shared basis and supported information

In _Using Language_ (1996), Clark distinguishes the **shared basis** from the information it supports. A father and son might each expect the father home at six because each left the other a note—but neither received the other's note. Matching expectations reached on these separate bases do not supply the shared justification each assumes. Clark's principle of justification concerns participants' belief that they have a proper shared basis for treating information as common ground. (p. 96)[^2]

The same basis can support different claims with different strength. Looking together at a shell can be excellent evidence that both see the shell, yet poor evidence that both know its age. Participants also distinguish types of supported information: mutual knowledge, belief, assumption, and perception are not interchangeable. (p. 98)[^2]

Individuals act on their own representations of common ground, and those representations can conflict. One person may take it as shared that the object is a snail shell, another that it is a conch shell; a later reference can expose the discrepancy. Shared information and an individual's belief about what is shared must therefore be distinguished. (pp. 96–98)[^2]

## Communal and personal sources

Clark distinguishes **communal common ground**, inferred from evidence of cultural-community membership, from **personal common ground**, grounded in joint experiences such as perceiving or acting together. These are sources of defeasible assumptions, not guarantees that everyone in a category has identical knowledge. (pp. 100, 112)[^2] His Sherlock Holmes example makes the publicity condition concrete: Holmes may infer from a man's calluses that he is a shoemaker, but that inference is not thereby common ground with the man. The evidence must itself be taken as mutually available in the relevant way. (p. 117)[^2]

For a tentative agent application, retain both an asserted shared fact and the interaction that warrants treating it as shared. A private memory of the user's occupation or preferred dataset is not automatically a shared basis for the next exchange. [[joint-salience|Joint salience]] concerns how participants choose a coordination solution against that basis; [[joint-action#activity-state-and-record|Activity state and record]] concerns what their interaction has placed on record.
