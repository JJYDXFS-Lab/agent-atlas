---
title: Common ground
---

Drawing on earlier accounts, Clark and Brennan describe common ground as participants' mutual knowledge, beliefs, and assumptions (p. 127).[^1] It provides a basis for coordinating what they mean and how they carry out a joint activity. The relevant notion is shared information as participants understand it, not merely a collection of facts available somewhere.

## Shared access and shared understanding

Two collaborators may both have a report without realizing that the other has read it. Conversely, they may jointly assume an outdated deadline. These hypothetical cases distinguish common ground from identical data access and from objective truth. On this reading, the beliefs and assumptions on which communication proceeds can be mistaken.

Suppose a user says “use the revised table.” Whether this reference works depends on which table the participants take to be mutually identifiable. Merely storing the table in [[agent-memory|Agent memory]], or inserting it into a [[context-window|Context window]], does not establish that mutual understanding.

Common ground is the shared basis; [[conversational-grounding|grounding]] is the collaborative process of establishing and updating it. Clark and Brennan distinguish coordinating the content of a joint activity from coordinating its process: coordinating on content presupposes common ground, while coordinating on process requires updating it moment by moment (p. 127).[^1] [[theory-of-mind|Theory of mind]] can involve beliefs about another participant's beliefs, but modeling those beliefs is not by itself evidence that something is mutually understood. This entry follows Clark and Brennan's communication account.

[^1]: Clark, Herbert H., and Susan E. Brennan. “Grounding in Communication.” 1991. In _Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition_, 127–149. The opening discussion, pp. 127–129, introduces common ground and the grounding criterion.
