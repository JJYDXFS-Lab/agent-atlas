# Asymmetric access to interactional resources (analyst term)

**Asymmetric access to interactional resources** is an analyst's label used in this Atlas for a recurring problem in Suchman's copier study; it is not presented as her named formal theory. Participants have unequal access to actions, histories, task circumstances, and the resources for interpreting or repairing an interaction. Machine recognition of an event does not settle its significance in the user's activity. (Chapters 8–9, pp. 116–118, 132–136)[^s]

## Access and relevance

The user can interpret an instruction through an unfolding practical project, while the machine's recognized events and encoded task account support a different range of inferences. A next instruction or repeated exchange may allow interaction to continue while the parties' understandings diverge. Suchman's analysis connects this problem to limits in the machine's access to the user's circumstances, rather than only to incorrect execution of an otherwise mutually understood plan. (Chapters 8–9, pp. 116–118, 132–136, 145–151)[^s]

The conclusions distinguish improving machine access, making limitations visible to users, and providing human or organizational support. These are different interventions. Additional sensing can help without removing the question of which circumstances are relevant. (Chapter 10, pp. 179–183)[^s]

## Boundaries

[[belief-state|Belief state]] can summarize information under a probabilistic model, including history-dependent information when appropriately represented. Suchman's case does not prove that POMDPs cannot represent histories. The comparison asks what a particular implementation makes accessible and how interactional significance is achieved, rather than declaring a formal impossibility.

[[horizon-of-observation|Horizon of observation]] focuses on detection and learning opportunities. [[conversational-grounding|Conversational grounding]] asks what evidence supports sufficient understanding. The access problem here is also narrower than the later political questions of difference, labor, and responsibility in [[human-machine-reconfiguration|Human–machine reconfiguration]].

## Contemporary application

An illustrative assistant might see a clicked control but lack the document state or the user's reason for clicking. A useful evaluation would distinguish recognized events, inferred task relevance, and available repair channels. This is an Atlas extrapolation: the historical copier's limitations are not evidence of a universal inability of modern language systems.
