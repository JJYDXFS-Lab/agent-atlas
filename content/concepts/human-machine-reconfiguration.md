# Human–machine reconfiguration

In Suchman's later work, **human–machine reconfiguration** directs inquiry toward how boundaries and capacities of humans and machines are enacted in particular arrangements. It asks whose work makes apparent autonomy possible, which differences are maintained or obscured, and who bears the consequences of the arrangement. This is a situated research orientation rather than a universal classification of entities. (Chapters 14–15, pp. 246–250, 283–286)[^s]

## Boundaries, difference, and invisible work

Examples including Cog/Kismet and the Prosthetic Head bring supporting arrangements, maintenance, and shared performance into view. The apparent autonomous actor depends on work that may disappear from the public presentation. Recognizing mutual constitution does not require treating all participants as the same or removing differences in power and responsibility. (Chapter 14, pp. 246–250; Chapter 15, pp. 268–270, 277–286)[^s]

A boundary is consequential: deciding what is inside the named system can also decide whose contribution counts and whose burdens remain outside the account. Suchman's argument does not supply a settled rule for who may determine every legitimate boundary. The responsibility question continues after an artefact is designed or labeled a collaborator. (pp. 283–286)[^s]

## Comparisons

[[distributed-cognitive-system|Distributed cognitive system]] explains propagation and transformation across people and media. Reconfiguration's attention to political differences and accountability is not captured simply by enlarging that unit. These are different emphases, not evidence that Suchman and Hutchins are mutually exclusive theories.

[[substitution-myth|Substitution myth]] challenges the assumption that other work remains unchanged after automation. Reconfiguration also examines how the identities and boundaries of the participants are produced. [[situated-action|Situated action]] supplies the earlier inquiry into the work of realizing plans; [[artifact-interface|Artifact interface]] provides a different, purpose-relative analytical boundary.

## Application

For an Atlas illustration, assess an apparently autonomous research assistant together with the people who maintain sources, correct output, configure access, and handle failures. Ask who receives credit and who absorbs the burden, as well as whether the task succeeds. These contemporary questions are extrapolations, not measured LLM effects. A permission system can constrain actions but cannot by itself settle the broader relational and political responsibilities discussed here.
