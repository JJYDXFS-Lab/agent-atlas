---
title: Least collaborative effort
---

The principle of least collaborative effort says that conversational participants try to minimize the work they jointly do from the initiation of a contribution to its mutual acceptance. Clark and Brennan restate the principle from Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs (1986); this entry follows their chapter rather than an independent reading of the earlier paper (pp. 134–135).[^1] Its unit is the joint contribution, including the work of establishing understanding, rather than the speaker's utterance alone.

## Why collaboration changes effort

An account that expects speakers to produce flawless utterances overlooks time pressure, errors, and speakers' limited knowledge of their addressee, which can make a proper utterance unattainable however much effort they spend. A provisional description offered for confirmation, an installment, or an invitation to complete an utterance can reduce the combined work compared with preparing a flawless presentation unaided. The chapter also interprets the preferences, reported by Schegloff, Jefferson, and Sacks (1977), for repairing one's own utterances and for initiating one's own repairs in terms of lower collaborative effort (pp. 134–135).[^1]

Extra turns have costs. Yet minimizing their number is not the principle: an additional exchange may save more work in formulation, misunderstanding, or later repair. Conversely, when producing messages and changing speakers are expensive, participants may try to accomplish more within each turn. Clark and Brennan use this reasoning to explain a reported telephone–keyboard contrast in an instruction task (pp. 145–147).[^1] See [[medium-constraints-on-grounding|Medium constraints on grounding]] for the costs and tradeoffs.

## Action, verification, and packaging

In _Using Language_ (1996), Clark makes the cost of closure explicit: effort includes both doing an action and obtaining evidence that it is complete. Individual least effort concerns an individual's action; joint closure requires evidence that the participants have succeeded together, and its economy concerns their joint effort. Saving the speaker work by leaving the listener uncertain need not save work overall. (pp. 222–226)[^2]

Packaging changes that cost. Very small contributions can make confirmation burdensome, while a long contribution can let an early misunderstanding grow and become harder to repair. Participants adjust the size to their skills and purposes. In Clark's address example, a listener repeats a street number incorrectly; a short correction and a fresh repetition settle that installment before the speaker continues. (pp. 235–238)[^2]

This reasoning supplies no unified numerical cost function. In discussing the choice among signaling methods, Clark explicitly leaves the metric of effort unanswered. Token count, elapsed time, repair frequency, and user attention might be useful measurements in an Atlas study, but combining them into one score would be an additional modeling choice, not Clark's result. (p. 187)[^2]

## Boundaries and related concepts

This is an explanatory principle about what participants try to minimize, not a claim that they calculate or attain a global minimum. Clark and Brennan use it to predict choices among available grounding techniques (p. 140).[^1] The aim remains reaching the criterion of sufficient understanding described in [[conversational-grounding#Grounding criterion|Conversational grounding]]; minimizing effort does not license abandoning that criterion.

As an Atlas comparison, [[satisficing|Satisficing]] concerns finding an alternative that meets an acceptability standard, whereas least collaborative effort concerns the combined work of a communicative contribution. A threshold for sufficient understanding does not make the two theories identical, and the comparison does not assert an intellectual lineage.

## Illustrative agent application

An assistant preparing a report might ask whether “the revised table” means the March revision before drafting around it. The clarification adds a turn but may avoid a long, unusable draft. This is an Atlas design interpretation of the human communication account, not an agent experiment reported in the chapter. Assess the user's effort as well as the assistant's; token count or response length alone does not measure their combined work. Evidence of understanding still does not authorize execution.
