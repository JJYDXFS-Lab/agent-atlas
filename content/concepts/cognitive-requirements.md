# Cognitive requirements

An AWACS weapons director must identify threats and maintain an understanding of a changing situation while using a cluttered display. In the project described by Crandall, Klein, and Hoffman, researchers investigated difficult decisions, possible errors, and the cues and patterns experienced operators used. Those findings informed interface recommendations focused on supporting difficult judgments. (Chapter 11, pp. 174–177)[^1]

**Cognitive requirements** are the challenging aspects of a task together with the reasons for their difficulty, the errors people make, and the strategies needed to address them. They connect an account of work to specific design, training, and evaluation questions. A broad aim such as “better situation awareness” still needs to be made concrete in the relevant task. (Chapter 14, pp. 235–236)[^1]

## From difficulty to design and evaluation

A requirement can concern attention, display clutter, memory, or interpretation, as well as missing information. In the AWACS example, detecting and tracking threats involves attention, screen clutter, memory, and interpreting a track's history. The authors describe a redesign and simulator evaluation, not a universal result for every display or operational setting. (Chapter 11, pp. 174–177)[^1]

Requirements can guide both initial design and acceptance criteria: which decisions must improve, under what conditions, and what performance would support that conclusion? The evidence and measures must remain specific to the work. (Chapter 14, pp. 235–236)[^1]

## Decision requirements table

A **decision requirements table (DRT)** organizes difficult decisions alongside relevant cues or information, strategies or practices, and potential pitfalls. The book's civil-law mediation example brings these elements together across phases of mediation. Such a table can consolidate one incident or findings across several records; it is a representation of analyzed evidence, not a direct recording of an expert's internal cognition. (Chapter 7, pp. 122–124)[^1]

The table is one way to express cognitive requirements; filling its columns does not by itself validate the analysis. [[problem-representation|Problem representation]] addresses the broader issue of how a representation changes what can be examined and done.

## Requirements can change

The authors recommend attending to relatively enduring cognitive challenges when designing for future work, while also discussing how technology changes tasks and expertise. A requirement is therefore a revisable judgment, not something guaranteed to survive a technological redesign unchanged. (Chapter 10, pp. 159–168)[^1] Compare [[substitution-myth|Substitution myth]] for why replacing a component can reorganize the work.

[[decision-premises|Decision premises]] concerns what governs a choice; [[artifact-interface|Artifact interface]] concerns fit among an artifact, its environment, and its purpose. Cognitive requirements instead specifies what makes the work cognitively difficult. [[problem-detection|Problem detection]] is one possible challenge to investigate.

For a hypothetical agent review tool, ask which discrepancies people struggle to recognize and which errors a proposed feature should help them catch. This is a research question, not evidence that human task-analysis findings validate a model's self-explanations.
