# Decision premises

**Decision premises** are the factual and value commitments from which a decision is reached in Simon's analysis. Organizational influence can shape an individual's action by determining some of these premises without prescribing every detail. Simon names authority, communication, training, efficiency, and identification (organizational loyalty) as modes of influence that can supply premises. He notes that these modes do not exclude one another. (Chapter VII, printed p. 177)[^1]

## Uses in the literature

Simon treats the scope of authority and the scope of discretion in terms of which premises are specified and which are left to the decision-maker. Different influences can constrain different parts of the same decision. His example of a composite decision shows that the officer who signs a contract may perform an almost ministerial function after many others have shaped its premises. An account of organization must therefore examine those earlier contributions as well as the final command or signature. (Chapter XI, printed pp. 306–309)[^1]

## Boundaries and related concepts

The [[fact-value-distinction|Fact–value distinction]] separates claims about consequences from preferred ends. A premise may be an uncertain estimate, not a verified fact. [[organizational-authority|Organizational authority]] is one way premises are accepted; it is not the only organizational influence. Information held in [[agent-memory|Agent memory]] becomes a decision premise only when it actually structures the choice; storage alone is not enough.

## Operational example

An illustrative delegated agent receives a spending limit and a required delivery date, then chooses a supplier using price and stock estimates. Record the source of each constraint, the evidence for each estimate, and the choices still left open. If estimates change, reconsider the resulting decision; the original conclusion was conditional on those estimates.

## Discretion and partial visibility

Compare [[joint-action#activity-state-and-record|Activity state and record]] when reading an interaction history, and [[joint-project|Joint project]] when asking what a response commits participants to. These links distinguish the course of an exchange from the premises shaping a choice; they do not turn a transcript into a complete decision history.

Simon locates discretion in the premises left unspecified, rather than treating it as an all-or-nothing property of the final decision-maker. His composite-decision discussion also limits what any participant can reconstruct about the whole decision. A record of the final signature cannot reveal all the contributions, exclusions, and anticipated reactions that shaped it. (Chapter XI, printed pp. 307–310)[^1] [[organizational-communication|Organizational communication]] develops the reporting and screening mechanisms through which some premises become available. Audit histories can supply partial evidence; they should not be described as complete influence histories.
