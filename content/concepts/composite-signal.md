# Composite signal

Someone points to a book and says that it belongs to them. The words and the pointing jointly identify what is being claimed about which object. Treating either as the whole message misses how the reference works. Clark uses such cases to explain **composite signals**, in which methods of signaling are combined. (pp. 165, 168; p. 391)[^1]

## Three methods of signaling

Clark distinguishes **describing-as**, using symbols to characterize something as a kind of thing; **indicating**, using an index to locate something; and **demonstrating**, using an icon to depict something. Saying “dog,” pointing to a bicycle, and showing how someone holds a teacup illustrate the respective methods. They are components that can be combined, not mutually exclusive categories of messages. (pp. 160, 168, 187)[^1]

Indicating requires more than producing a pointer. The index must be in the participants' joint focus of attention, locate the object in space and time through a physical connection, and specify it under a description; the addressee must be able to work this out from current common ground. A gesture toward a book must be construed as locating the book, not merely its color or some other feature. (p. 165)[^1]

## Manifesting actions

Placing goods on a counter can be an ordinary physical action. Doing so conspicuously at the right moment in response to a clerk can also manifest which goods one wants to buy. The signaling contribution lies in how the action is made recognizable for the other participant, including stylization and timing. Not every visible action is thereby a signal. (pp. 167–168)[^1]

## Selective depiction

A demonstration need not reproduce everything about its subject. Clark distinguishes **depictive** aspects intended to represent it, **supportive** aspects needed to perform the demonstration, **annotative** aspects that comment on it, and **incidental** aspects. In a demonstration of drinking tea, hand movements may depict the action while an imaginary cup merely supports the performance; exaggeration can supply commentary. The recipient must distinguish what is being depicted from what is not. (p. 173)[^1]

The available mix depends on purpose, medium, and effort: a gesture possible face to face may be unavailable over a telephone, while some actions are easier to demonstrate than to describe. Clark explicitly leaves the metric of effort unresolved. (p. 187)[^1] See [[medium-constraints-on-grounding|Medium constraints on grounding]] and [[least-collaborative-effort|Least collaborative effort]].

## Shared interfaces

In a tentative Atlas analogy, an explanation beside a highlighted table cell and a worked example may combine several signaling methods. A `file:line` string alone may only describe a location symbolically; treating a particular interface anchor as indicating requires an account of how both participants attend to and locate its target. A worked example also needs to distinguish its intended lesson from incidental values. These are questions for interface analysis, not a classification of every agent output as describing-as.

[[pragmatics|Pragmatics]] places interpretation in context. [[layering-in-language-use|Layering in language use]] asks whose action or voice a quotation or demonstration represents.
